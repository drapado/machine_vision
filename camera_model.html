Online reader for Machine Vision at WUR - TA2: Camera model - From 3D to 2D

Author: David Rapado-Rincon

This page gives a compact walk-through of the camera model from Tutorial A2. We start with the cleanest possible projection model and then add the ingredients real cameras need.

To map a 3D world point onto a 2D image point, we go through four stages:

  1. Transform the world point X̃world into the camera coordinate system. The result is X̃cam.
  2. Project the 3D point onto an ideal 2D sensor plane.
  3. Move from the sensor plane to the image plane. This is where the principal point offset and pixel dimensions enter the model.
  4. Account for departures from the ideal linear model. This includes lens distortion and other non-linear effects.

1. Step 2 - Ideal projection from 3D to 2D

Start with the simplest case: the 3D point is already expressed in camera coordinates, and the camera behaves ideally. The question is then just where that point lands on the image plane.

  • Conceptual setup: The camera center, or center of projection, is at (0,0,0), and the image plane is given by Z = f.
  • Projection mechanism: A 3D point X = (X,Y,Z)T maps to the place where the line through X and the camera center meets the image plane.
  • Geometric derivation: Similar triangles give the projected point (fX/Z, fY/Z).
(1) \[\begin{bmatrix}X \\ Y \\ Z\end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix}\frac{fX}{Z} \\ \frac{fY}{Z}\end{bmatrix}\]

1.1 Using homogeneous coordinates

Homogeneous coordinates let us write the projection in matrix form. The perspective division is still there, but the linear part becomes much easier to handle.

(2) \[\begin{bmatrix}X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix}fX \\ fY \\ Z\end{bmatrix} = P\begin{bmatrix}X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1\end{bmatrix}\]
(3) \[P = \operatorname{diag}(f,f,1)[I\mid 0] = \begin{bmatrix}f & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & f & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\]

2. Step 3 - From sensor to camera (Principal point offset)

The origin of the image-plane coordinates does not have to sit exactly at the principal point. Let (px, py)T denote the principal point in image coordinates.

(4) \[\begin{bmatrix}X \\ Y \\ Z\end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix}\frac{fX}{Z} + p_x \\ \frac{fY}{Z} + p_y\end{bmatrix}\]
(5) \[\begin{bmatrix}X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix}fX + Zp_x \\ fY + Zp_y \\ Z\end{bmatrix}\]
(6) \[K = \begin{bmatrix}f & 0 & p_x \\ 0 & f & p_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\]
(7) \[x = K[I\mid 0]X_{\mathrm{cam}}\]

3. Step 1 - Camera rotation and translation with respect to the world

So far everything lived in the camera frame. In practice, 3D points are usually given in a separate world frame, so we need a rigid transformation made of rotation and translation.

(8) \[\tilde{X}_{\mathrm{cam}} = R(\tilde{X} - \tilde{C})\]
(9) \[X_{\mathrm{cam}} = \begin{bmatrix}R & -R\tilde{C} \\ 0^T & 1\end{bmatrix}X\]
(10) \[x = KR[I\mid -\tilde{C}]X\]
(11) \[P = K[R\mid t],\quad t = -R\tilde{C}\]

4. Digital sensors - pixel space

Real digital cameras measure position in pixels rather than in metric sensor coordinates. They can also have different pixel densities along x and y, so the calibration matrix has to reflect that.

If mx and my are the pixel densities in x and y, the calibration matrix becomes:

(12) \[K = \begin{bmatrix}f_x & 0 & x_0 \\ 0 & f_y & y_0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\]

Here, fx = f·mx, fy = f·my, x0 = px·mx, and y0 = py·my.

The geometry stays the same; this version simply expresses it in pixel units.
