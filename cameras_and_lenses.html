Cameras and Lenses

1. Recording Light with Sensors

A digital image sensor is a grid of photodetectors. Each detector turns incoming photons into an electrical signal that we can measure. More photons means a stronger signal.

In the demo below, photons land on the small square detectors over time. Use the slider to change the collection time.

Longer collection times let more photons reach the detector, so the image gets brighter. If too few photons are collected, the image is underexposed. If we collect for too long, it becomes overexposed.

One thing to notice is that the raw image is black and white. Even though light has different wavelengths, a bare photodetector mostly measures how much light arrives, not its color. To capture color, we place tiny filters over the pixels so each one mainly passes red, green, or blue light. The most common layout is the Bayer filter pattern:

The Bayer pattern is a 2x2 block with two green filters, one red filter, and one blue filter. Other layouts exist, such as X-Trans, but Bayer is still the standard. Each filter passes only a limited part of the spectrum to the detector below:

3. Pinhole Camera

We need a way to limit which rays reach the sensor. The simplest idea is a box with a tiny hole in front. The first slider changes the hole diameter, and the second changes the distance from the opening to the sensor:

That gives us a pinhole camera. The image flips because the rays cross at the opening, so the scene appears upside down and left-right reversed. The next figures show that geometry.

We define the frustum of a camera as the volume of space visible to the camera. It is determined by the field of view, the near and far clipping planes, and the aspect ratio of the image or sensor.

In a pinhole camera, sharpness depends on the hole size. A smaller hole gives a sharper image, but it also lets in less light.

Figure 15 - Solid angle of pinhole

The next figure shows what changes on the sensor when the hole size changes:

Up to this point we used an ideal sensor that can wait as long as needed for light. A real sensor has limits. If the hole is too large, rays from one scene point spread over several pixels and the image gets blurry. If the hole is too small, the image sharpens but becomes dark because less light gets through. We can compensate with a longer exposure, but that introduces motion blur when the scene or camera moves.

4. Thin Lens Model

The pinhole camera makes the trade-off obvious: either gather more light or keep the image sharp. A lens helps with both. It collects more light from the scene and bends those rays so points we care about can meet again at the sensor. In simplified form, that looks like this:

If we place the sensor where those rays meet, the image is sharp. Cameras do this with lenses, and the simplest model that captures the idea is the thin lens.

A thin lens is an idealized piece of transparent material, usually illustrated as glass with two spherical surfaces. Real lenses are more complicated, but this model is enough to explain the basic geometry:

The point where parallel rays meet is the focal point. In the next figure you can move the ray origin and see how that point shifts:

The main characteristic of a lens is its focal length. This is the distance from the center of the lens to the focal point when incoming rays are parallel to the optical axis. Two things set that distance: the curvature of the lens surfaces and the refractive index of the lens material:

4.1 Focus and Bokeh

When the sensor sits at the right distance, a scene point is focused onto a point on the sensor. Move the sensor away from that position and the rays no longer meet cleanly, so the image blurs. 4.2 Aperture

There is one more control that matters a lot: aperture. The aperture is the opening that lets light through the lens. It changes both the amount of light reaching the sensor and the depth of field. Start by looking at how the width of the light cone affects sharpness:

The aperture is set by the diaphragm, a ring of blades that opens or closes the lens opening. The number and shape of those blades matter. More blades usually make the opening more circular, which tends to produce smoother-looking bokeh. They also explain why out-of-focus highlights sometimes look polygonal: the blur inherits the shape of the opening.

A smaller aperture narrows the light cone and increases depth of field, so more of the scene appears sharp. A larger aperture widens the cone and reduces depth of field.

Notice that changing focus by moving the sensor also slightly changes the field of view. That effect is called focus breathing.
